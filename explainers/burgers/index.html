Burgers' equation: how a smooth wave breaks

The simplest equation in which a perfectly smooth initial condition turns into a discontinuity in finite time, and the simplest place to see what viscosity is really for.

Start with a single rule

Imagine a line of particles, each carrying a value \(u\), and each moving with speed equal to its own value. Nothing pushes on any particle, so the value it carries never changes. That is the whole model. Written down, "\(u\) is constant along a path moving at speed \(u\)" is

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0 .$$

The left side is just the rate of change of \(u\) seen by an observer riding along at speed \(u\). Setting it to zero says that observer sees nothing change.

The paths themselves are called characteristics. A particle that starts at \(x_0\) keeps the value \(u_0(x_0)\) forever, so its path is a straight line:

$$x(t) = x_0 + u_0(x_0)\,t .$$

The solution is therefore known exactly, as long as each point \(x\) is reached by only one such line: \(u(x,t) = u_0(x_0)\), where \(x_0\) is the starting point of the line that arrives at \(x\).

Why it has to break

Where \(u_0\) decreases, a fast particle sits behind a slow one. The fast one catches up. Follow two neighbouring particles that start a distance \(\delta x_0\) apart. After time \(t\) their separation is

$$\delta x(t) = \big(1 + u_0'(x_0)\,t\big)\,\delta x_0 .$$

If \(u_0'(x_0)<0\), this separation shrinks to zero at \(t = -1/u_0'(x_0)\). The first place this happens is where the initial profile slopes down most steeply, which gives the breaking time

$$t_b = \frac{1}{\max_{x_0}\big(-u_0'(x_0)\big)} .$$

At \(t_b\) the gradient \(u_x\) becomes infinite. Past it, the straight-line construction places several values of \(u\) at the same \(x\). What replaces the multivalued profile: a shock

The equation above came from a conservation law. Writing it in conservative form,

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(\frac{u^2}{2}\Big) = 0,$$

says that the total \(\int u\,dx\) in any interval changes only by the flux \(u^2/2\) through its ends. This statement still makes sense when \(u\) jumps. Apply it to a small box around a jump from \(u_L\) on the left to \(u_R\) on the right, moving at speed \(s\). In time \(dt\) the box gains \(u_L^2/2-u_R^2/2\) through its walls, and the jump sweeping through converts a length \(s\,dt\) from \(u_R\) to \(u_L\). Balancing the two gives the Rankine–Hugoniot condition

$$s = \frac{u_L^2/2 - u_R^2/2}{u_L - u_R} = \frac{u_L + u_R}{2}.$$

The shock moves at the average of the states on either side. Integral conservation picks the position of the jump, which is why the shock cuts the S-shaped curve so that the areas it removes on either side are equal.

Not every jump is allowed. A jump is admissible only if characteristics run into it from both sides, which requires \(u_L > u_R\). When \(u_L < u_R\), characteristics fan apart instead and the jump opens into a smooth ramp, a rarefaction wave, \(u = (x-x_0)/t\). The two step problems in the simulation show exactly this asymmetry.

What viscosity does

Real fluids diffuse momentum. Adding that effect gives the viscous Burgers equation,

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \nu\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}.$$

Steepening concentrates gradients; diffusion smooths them at a rate that grows with the gradient. The two balance in a travelling front. Looking for a profile \(u(x - st)\) and integrating once gives \(\nu u' = \tfrac12 (u-u_L)(u-u_R)\), whose solution is

$$u = s - \frac{\Delta}{2}\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\Delta\,(x - st)}{4\nu}\Big), \qquad \Delta = u_L - u_R .$$

The front has width of order \(4\nu/\Delta\), so it sharpens as \(\nu \to 0\). Its speed is \(s=(u_L+u_R)/2\), the Rankine–Hugoniot speed, independent of \(\nu\). The inviscid shock is the limit of these viscous fronts, and that limit is how the entropy condition is justified.

The energy puzzle

Multiplying the equation by \(u\) and integrating over a periodic domain gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int \tfrac12 u^2\,dx = -\nu\int u_x^2\,dx .$$

Before \(t_b\), gradients are bounded, so as \(\nu\to 0\) the right side vanishes and energy is conserved. After \(t_b\), the gradient inside the front grows like \(\Delta/\nu\) over a width proportional to \(\nu\). Integrating the tanh profile shows that the two factors cancel exactly: each shock removes energy at the rate

$$\nu\int u_x^2\,dx = \frac{\Delta^3}{12},$$

whatever \(\nu\) is. The dissipation does not go to zero with the viscosity. This is the one-dimensional cartoon of the dissipative anomaly in turbulence, and the reason a coarse simulation that cannot resolve the front still has to remove the right amount of energy there. That is the job of a numerical scheme's built-in dissipation, or of an explicit subgrid model.

About the numerics. The simulation uses a finite-volume method: second-order MUSCL reconstruction with a minmod limiter, the exact Godunov flux for \(u^2/2\), central differences for \(\nu u_{xx}\), and second-order strong-stability-preserving Runge–Kutta in time. The time step obeys both the advective limit \(\Delta t \le 0.45\,\Delta x/\max|u|\) and the diffusive limit \(\Delta t \le 0.25\,\Delta x^2/\nu\). Why such limits exist is the subject of the finite-difference stability explainer.
