Eigenvectors: the directions a transformation keeps

Under most transformations almost every vector is knocked off the line it started on. The few directions that are not knocked off are the eigenvectors, and they are the simplest way to describe what the transformation does.

Most vectors get knocked off their line

Pick a vector \(\vec v\) and think about its span, the line through the origin and \(\vec v\). Apply a transformation and look at \(A\vec v\). For most vectors and most transformations, \(A\vec v\) is no longer on that line: the vector has been rotated as well as stretched.

A few special directions behave differently. Vectors along them stay on their own line, and are only stretched, shrunk or flipped:

$$A\vec v = \lambda\,\vec v, \qquad \vec v \neq \vec 0.$$

Such a \(\vec v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\), and the factor \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue. A negative \(\lambda\) flips the vector to the other side of the origin but keeps it on the same line, and \(\lambda = 0\) means that direction is crushed to the origin: it is the null space from chapter 5.

An example by hand

Take \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). The first column says \(\hat\imath\) lands on \((3, 0)\): still on the horizontal axis, three times as long. So \(\hat\imath\) is an eigenvector with \(\lambda = 3\). Less obviously,

$$A\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 + 1 \\ 0 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},$$

so the diagonal direction \((-1, 1)\) is an eigenvector with \(\lambda = 2\). Every other direction gets turned. This is the first preset in the visualizer.

Finding them: a question about the determinant

Rewrite the eigenvector equation as

$$A\vec v - \lambda\vec v = \vec 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (A - \lambda I)\,\vec v = \vec 0 .$$

So \(\vec v\) must be a nonzero vector that the matrix \(A - \lambda I\) sends to the origin. By chapter 5 that happens exactly when \(A - \lambda I\) squishes the plane, that is, when

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0 .$$

For a 2×2 matrix this is \((a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = 0\), which expands to

$$\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda + \det(A) = 0, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(A) = a + d .$$

For the example, \(\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 2)\), giving the two eigenvalues found by hand. Each root \(\lambda\) is then turned into a direction by finding the null space of \(A - \lambda I\).

Three ways it can turn out

Two real eigenvalues. Two distinct eigen-directions, as in the example. A symmetric matrix (\(b = c\)) always lands here or in the uniform-scale case, and its eigen-directions are perpendicular. Projections and reflections across a line through the origin are symmetric, and their eigenvalues can be read off the picture: vectors on the line are untouched (\(\lambda = 1\)), while vectors perpendicular to it are crushed by a projection (\(\lambda = 0\)) and flipped by a reflection (\(\lambda = -1\)).

Complex eigenvalues. A 90° rotation turns every vector, so it has no real eigenvectors. The algebra agrees: \(\lambda^2 + 1 = 0\) has the roots \(\pm i\). Whenever a real 2×2 matrix has complex eigenvalues \(\alpha \pm \beta i\) with \(\beta \neq 0\), it turns every direction.

A repeated eigenvalue. The shear \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has \((\lambda - 1)^2 = 0\), one eigenvalue counted twice, but only one eigen-direction, the horizontal axis. At the other extreme a uniform scaling \(2I\) also has \(\lambda = 2\) twice, and there every vector is an eigenvector.

Why they matter: an eigenbasis

Suppose \(A\) has two independent eigenvectors \(\vec e_1, \vec e_2\). Use them as a basis (chapter 1) and write \(\vec v = a\,\vec e_1 + b\,\vec e_2\). Then applying \(A\) is just scaling each coordinate:

$$A\vec v = a\lambda_1\vec e_1 + b\lambda_2\vec e_2, \qquad A^n\vec v = a\lambda_1^{\,n}\vec e_1 + b\lambda_2^{\,n}\vec e_2 .$$

In that basis the matrix is diagonal, and repeated application, which would otherwise mean multiplying matrices over and over, becomes raising two numbers to a power. If \(|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2|\) and \(a \neq 0\), the first term dominates as \(n\) grows, so the direction of \(A^n\vec v\) approaches the line of \(\vec e_1\). The "Apply A again" button shows exactly this. As an algorithm it is called power iteration, and it is how dominant eigenvectors of very large matrices are computed in practice.

A detail of the animation. "Play" shows \((1-t)I + tA\) for \(t\) from 0 to 1. Every eigenvector of \(A\) is an eigenvector of each of these frames too: \(\big((1-t)I + tA\big)\vec v = \big((1-t) + t\lambda\big)\vec v\). So vectors on the magenta lines stay on them for the whole animation, not just at the end.
