Linear algebra · Chapter 2 of 6

A matrix is where î and ĵ land

A matrix is not a table that happens to multiply vectors. It records where a transformation sends two arrows, and that is enough to know where it sends every arrow.

A function that moves the whole plane

A transformation takes each input vector to an output vector. It is just a function, but "transformation" suggests the right picture: instead of following one arrow, imagine every point of the plane moving to its new position at once. Drawing the grid lines after the move shows the whole function in one image.

What makes a transformation linear

Algebraically, \(L\) is linear when it respects the two operations from chapter 1:

$$L(\vec v + \vec w) = L(\vec v) + L(\vec w), \qquad L(c\,\vec v) = c\,L(\vec v).$$

Geometrically this means the origin stays put, and the grid lines remain straight, parallel and evenly spaced. Rotations, reflections, stretches and shears all qualify. A translation does not, because it moves the origin. Neither does anything that bends a grid line or bunches lines more closely on one side.

Two arrows decide everything

Here is the key step. Any input can be written in terms of the basis, \(\vec v = x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath\). Apply \(L\) and use the two rules:

$$L(\vec v) = L(x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath) = x\,L(\hat\imath) + y\,L(\hat\jmath).$$

The output is the same combination of wherever \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) landed. So once you know those two landing spots, you know where every vector goes. Suppose \(\hat\imath\) lands on \((a, c)\) and \(\hat\jmath\) on \((b, d)\). Then

$$L\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{bmatrix}.$$

Writing the two landing spots side by side as columns gives the matrix of \(L\):

$$\begin{bmatrix} \color{#b7ff3c}{a} & \color{#ff7a45}{b} \\ \color{#b7ff3c}{c} & \color{#ff7a45}{d} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x\begin{bmatrix} \color{#b7ff3c}{a} \\ \color{#b7ff3c}{c} \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} \color{#ff7a45}{b} \\ \color{#ff7a45}{d} \end{bmatrix}.$$

That is the definition of a matrix times a vector, and the reason behind it. The first column is where \(\hat\imath\) lands, the second is where \(\hat\jmath\) lands, and multiplying by a vector means "take that combination of the columns".

Reading a few matrices

Rotation by 90°. \(\hat\imath\) swings up to \((0, 1)\) and \(\hat\jmath\) swings left to \((-1, 0)\), so the matrix is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\). Nothing to memorize: you can read off where the arrows went.

Shear. \(\hat\imath\) stays put and \(\hat\jmath\) tips over to \((1, 1)\): \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\). Horizontal lines slide sideways by an amount proportional to their height.

Squish. If the two columns are parallel, say \((1, 0.5)\) and \((2, 1)\), every output \(x\,\text{col}_1 + y\,\text{col}_2\) lies on their common line. The 2D plane is flattened onto 1D, and chapter 5 is about what that costs.

About the animation. "Play" slides each matrix entry linearly from the identity to its target, so frame \(t\) shows the transformation \((1-t)I + tA\). The intermediate frames are only a way to watch the motion, not part of the transformation. For a rotation they briefly shrink the plane: the entries travel in straight lines rather than around a circle, so halfway to a 90° turn the frame is a 45° turn combined with a shrink by \(1/\sqrt2\).
