Linear algebra · Chapter 4 of 6

The determinant measures how areas scale

Every linear transformation stretches or squishes areas by the same factor everywhere in the plane. That factor, with a sign for orientation, is the determinant.

Every area scales by the same factor

Watch the unit square, the one spanned by \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\). A linear transformation sends it to the parallelogram spanned by the two columns. Because grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced, every grid square lands on a translated copy of that same parallelogram. So every grid square's area is multiplied by the same number.

Any region can be approximated by small grid squares, as finely as you like, so its area is multiplied by that number too. One number describes the effect on all areas, and it is the area of the image of the unit square. That number is the determinant, \(\det A\).

A sign for orientation

Some transformations flip the plane over, like turning a sheet of paper face down. You can detect this from \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\). Before the transformation, \(\hat\jmath\) is a quarter-turn counterclockwise from \(\hat\imath\). If after the transformation the image of \(\hat\jmath\) is clockwise from the image of \(\hat\imath\), orientation has been reversed, and the determinant is defined to be negative. Its absolute value is still the area factor.

Between positive and negative sits zero. A determinant of zero means the unit square was flattened into a segment or a point: the transformation squishes the plane onto a line or onto the origin, and all area is lost.

Where \(ad - bc\) comes from

Write \(D(\vec u, \vec w)\) for the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vec u\) and \(\vec w\): positive when \(\vec w\) is counterclockwise from \(\vec u\), negative when it is clockwise. Three facts are enough to compute it.

  • \(D(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath) = 1\): the unit square.
  • \(D(\vec u, \vec u) = 0\): a parallelogram with two equal sides is flat.
  • \(D\) is linear in each argument. Take \(\vec u\) as the base. The area is the length of the base times the height of \(\vec w\) measured perpendicular to it, with a sign for which side \(\vec w\) is on. That signed height is \(\hat n\cdot\vec w\), where \(\hat n\) is the unit vector a quarter-turn counterclockwise from \(\vec u\), so \(D(\vec u, \vec w) = |\vec u|\,(\hat n \cdot \vec w)\), which is linear in \(\vec w\). The same argument with \(\vec w\) as the base gives linearity in \(\vec u\).

The second and third facts together give antisymmetry: expanding \(0 = D(\vec u + \vec w, \vec u + \vec w)\) leaves \(D(\vec u, \vec w) + D(\vec w, \vec u) = 0\). Now write the columns in terms of the basis, \(\vec u = a\hat\imath + c\hat\jmath\) and \(\vec w = b\hat\imath + d\hat\jmath\), and expand:

$$D(\vec u, \vec w) = ab\,\underbrace{D(\hat\imath, \hat\imath)}_{0} + ad\,\underbrace{D(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath)}_{1} + cb\,\underbrace{D(\hat\jmath, \hat\imath)}_{-1} + cd\,\underbrace{D(\hat\jmath, \hat\jmath)}_{0} = ad - bc.$$ $$\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc.$$

The \(ad\) term is the rectangle you would get if \(\hat\imath\) only stretched horizontally and \(\hat\jmath\) only vertically. The \(bc\) term is the correction for how much each column leans toward the other's axis.

Determinants multiply

Apply \(A\) and then \(B\). Areas are first multiplied by \(\det A\), then by \(\det B\). The composition \(BA\) therefore multiplies areas by the product:

$$\det(BA) = \det(B)\,\det(A).$$

From the entry formula this is an unpleasant algebra exercise; from the area picture it is one sentence. It also shows that if either step squishes the plane, so does the whole composition.
