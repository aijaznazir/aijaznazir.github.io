The singular value decomposition: rotate, stretch, rotate

Every matrix, square or not, does the same three things: it turns the input space, stretches it along perpendicular axes, and turns the result into place. The stretch factors, the singular values, say how big, how flat and how compressible a matrix is.

Every matrix turns a circle into an ellipse

Apply a 2×2 matrix \(A\) to every vector on the unit circle. Because grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, the circle lands on an ellipse, possibly flattened into a segment. An ellipse has a longest radius and a shortest one, at right angles to each other. Call their lengths

$$\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge 0 .$$

These are the singular values of \(A\). The directions of the ellipse's axes are unit vectors \(\vec u_1, \vec u_2\), and the input vectors that land on them are unit vectors \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2\) on the circle:

$$A\vec v_1 = \sigma_1\vec u_1, \qquad A\vec v_2 = \sigma_2\vec u_2 .$$

The surprising part is that \(\vec v_1\) and \(\vec v_2\) are also perpendicular. So every matrix, however it shears or skews the grid, sends some pair of perpendicular directions to another pair of perpendicular directions, stretched by \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\).

Why a perpendicular pair always survives

Here is a two-line argument in the plane. Let \(\vec v(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and \(\vec v^{\perp}(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), a quarter-turn further on. Measure how far their images are from perpendicular:

$$f(\theta) = A\vec v(\theta)\cdot A\vec v^{\perp}(\theta).$$

Turning by a further quarter-turn sends \(\vec v\) to \(\vec v^{\perp}\) and \(\vec v^{\perp}\) to \(-\vec v\), so \(f(\theta + \pi/2) = -f(\theta)\). A continuous function that changes sign must pass through zero, so at some \(\theta\) the images are perpendicular. In higher dimensions the same fact comes from the symmetric matrix \(A^{T}A\), below.

Rotate, stretch, rotate

Collect the vectors as columns, \(V = [\,\vec v_1\;\vec v_2\,]\) and \(U = [\,\vec u_1\;\vec u_2\,]\), and put the singular values on a diagonal. The two equations above say \(AV = U\Sigma\). Since \(V\) has orthonormal columns, \(V^{-1} = V^{T}\), and so

$$A = U\,\Sigma\,V^{T}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Read right to left as in chapter 3, this is a three-step recipe for any matrix:

  • \(V^{T}\) rotates the plane so that \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2\) line up with \(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath\);
  • \(\Sigma\) stretches along the axes, by \(\sigma_1\) horizontally and \(\sigma_2\) vertically;
  • \(U\) rotates the axes onto \(\vec u_1, \vec u_2\).

Orthogonal matrices cannot change lengths or angles, so all of the stretching is concentrated in \(\Sigma\). If \(\det A < 0\), one of the two orthogonal steps must also include a reflection; the visualizer puts it in \(U\). Since \(\det U\) and \(\det V\) are \(\pm 1\), the determinant becomes \(\det A = \pm\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\): the area factor from chapter 4 is the product of the two stretches.

Things to try
  • Play the three steps for the shear. A shear looks like pure sliding, yet it is a rotation, a stretch by the golden ratio 1.618 in one direction and a squeeze by 0.618 in the other, and another rotation.
  • Drag \(\vec x\) around the circle. Its image is longest at \(\pm\vec v_1\) (length \(\sigma_1\)) and shortest at \(\pm\vec v_2\) (length \(\sigma_2\)).
  • Pick "symmetric": \(\vec u_i = \vec v_i\), and the singular vectors are the eigenvectors from chapter 7.
  • Pick "rank 1": \(\sigma_2 = 0\), and the ellipse is flattened to a segment along the column space.

How to compute it: the matrix \(A^{T}A\)

Lengths are easiest to handle squared. For any input \(\vec x\),

$$|A\vec x|^2 = (A\vec x)\cdot(A\vec x) = \vec x^{T}\!\left(A^{T}A\right)\vec x .$$

The matrix \(A^{T}A\) is symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal set of eigenvectors (the spectral theorem). Its eigenvalues are never negative: if \(A^{T}A\vec v = \lambda\vec v\) with \(|\vec v| = 1\), then \(\lambda = \vec v^{T}A^{T}A\vec v = |A\vec v|^2 \ge 0\). So:

  • the right singular vectors \(\vec v_i\) are the orthonormal eigenvectors of \(A^{T}A\);
  • the singular values are \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\), the lengths \(|A\vec v_i|\);
  • the left singular vectors are \(\vec u_i = A\vec v_i / \sigma_i\) for each \(\sigma_i > 0\).

The \(\vec u_i\) are orthonormal automatically: \(\vec u_i\cdot\vec u_j = \vec v_i^{T}A^{T}A\vec v_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \lambda_j\,\vec v_i\cdot\vec v_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j)\), which is 1 when \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise. Nothing here needs \(A\) to be square. An \(m\times n\) matrix has \(A = U\Sigma V^{T}\) with \(U\) of size \(m\times m\), \(V\) of size \(n\times n\), and \(\Sigma\) an \(m\times n\) matrix that is zero except for \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0\) down its diagonal.

This is the key difference from eigenvectors. Eigenvectors may fail to exist (a rotation), may be too few (a shear), and need not be perpendicular; they also only make sense for square matrices. Singular vectors always exist, always come as orthonormal bases, and use two different bases, one for the inputs and one for the outputs. For a symmetric matrix the two ideas agree: the singular values are \(|\lambda_i|\) and the singular vectors are eigenvectors.

In practice no one forms \(A^{T}A\): squaring the matrix squares its condition number and loses accuracy. Library routines such as MATLAB's svd work on \(A\) directly. The visualizers here use one such method, one-sided Jacobi rotations, which applies rotations to pairs of columns of \(A\) until all its columns are orthogonal.

The SVD and the four subspaces

Let \(r\) be the number of nonzero singular values. Then \(A\vec v_i = \sigma_i\vec u_i\) is nonzero for \(i \le r\) and zero for \(i > r\), and the four subspaces of chapter 6 can be read straight off the two bases:

SubspaceOrthonormal basis from the SVDDimension
\(\operatorname{Row}(A)\)\(\vec v_1, \ldots, \vec v_r\)\(r\)
\(\operatorname{Nul}(A)\)\(\vec v_{r+1}, \ldots, \vec v_n\)\(n - r\)
\(\operatorname{Col}(A)\)\(\vec u_1, \ldots, \vec u_r\)\(r\)
\(\operatorname{Nul}(A^{T})\)\(\vec u_{r+1}, \ldots, \vec u_m\)\(m - r\)

So the rank is the number of nonzero singular values, and the SVD hands over all four subspaces at once, each with a perpendicular basis. In floating point "nonzero" means "above a tolerance": MATLAB's rank counts the singular values larger than a small multiple of \(\sigma_1\), which is more reliable than counting pivots after rounding errors.

A sum of rank-one pieces

Multiply out \(U\Sigma V^{T}\) column by column and the SVD becomes a sum:

$$A = \sigma_1\,\vec u_1\vec v_1^{T} + \sigma_2\,\vec u_2\vec v_2^{T} + \cdots + \sigma_r\,\vec u_r\vec v_r^{T}.$$

Each term \(\vec u_i\vec v_i^{T}\) is a rank-one matrix, an outer product, and the terms come in decreasing order of importance. Keeping only the first \(k\) gives \(A_k\). The Eckart–Young theorem says that no matrix of rank \(k\) is closer to \(A\), measured either by the largest stretch of the difference or by the Frobenius norm (the root of the sum of squared entries). The error is known exactly:

$$\|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \cdots + \sigma_r^2}, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}.$$

How fast the singular values fall decides how compressible \(A\) is. Storing \(A_k\) takes \(k\) vectors \(\vec u_i\), \(k\) vectors \(\vec v_i\) and \(k\) numbers, so \(k(m + n + 1)\) numbers instead of \(mn\).

Where the SVD is used

Compression and low-rank models. The panel above is the whole idea: keep the leading terms and discard the rest, knowing the error exactly. The same truncation is used to compress large data sets and to fill in missing entries of rating tables in recommender systems, where a low-rank model captures "taste" in a handful of directions.

Principal component analysis (PCA). Put \(N\) data points as the rows of a matrix and subtract the mean of each column. The right singular vectors are the principal directions, the directions of greatest spread, and \(\sigma_i^2/(N-1)\) is the variance of the data along \(\vec v_i\). Projecting onto the first few \(\vec v_i\) is the standard way to reduce the dimension of data.

Reduced-order models of flows (POD). In fluid dynamics, store snapshots of a simulated flow at many times as the columns of a matrix. Its left singular vectors are the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) modes: the spatial patterns that capture the most energy, in order. Projecting the governing equations onto the first few modes gives a reduced-order model that is far cheaper to run. (The vortex wake above compresses a single snapshot treated as a matrix, which shows the decay of singular values but is not POD itself.)

Least squares and the pseudo-inverse. When \(A\vec x = \vec b\) has no solution (chapter 5), the best compromise uses \(A^{+} = V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}\), where \(\Sigma^{+}\) inverts the nonzero \(\sigma_i\) and leaves the zeros alone. Then \(\vec x = A^{+}\vec b\) lands as close to \(\vec b\) as possible, and is the shortest such \(\vec x\).

Conditioning. For a square invertible \(A\), the condition number \(\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n\) measures how much relative errors in \(\vec b\) can be amplified in the solution of \(A\vec x = \vec b\). A thin ellipse in the first visualizer means a large \(\kappa\): some directions are stretched far more than others, and the system is sensitive to small errors.
