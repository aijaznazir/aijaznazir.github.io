Linear algebra · Chapter 1 of 7

Vectors, span and basis

What a list of numbers means as an arrow, what it means to combine arrows, and why two of them are usually enough to reach every point of the plane.

An arrow, and a list of two numbers

In this series a vector in the plane is an arrow whose tail sits at the origin. Two numbers describe it, its coordinates:

$$\vec v = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

reads as an instruction: walk 3 units along the horizontal axis, then 2 units up. Because every arrow starts at the origin, an arrow and the point at its tip determine each other. Later chapters switch freely between the two pictures: arrows when there are only a few vectors, points when there are many.

The only two things you can do

Add. To form \(\vec v + \vec w\), slide \(\vec w\) so that its tail sits at the tip of \(\vec v\); the sum is the arrow to where you end up. In coordinates this is entry by entry, because the horizontal steps and the vertical steps of the two walks can be taken in any order:

$$\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 + w_1 \\ v_2 + w_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Scale. Multiplying by a number \(c\) stretches the arrow by the factor \(c\), and a negative \(c\) also flips it to point the other way. Numbers used this way are called scalars, because scaling is what they do.

Everything "linear" in linear algebra is built from these two operations.

Coordinates are hidden scalars

Name the two unit arrows along the axes \(\hat\imath = (1, 0)\) and \(\hat\jmath = (0, 1)\). Then

$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 3\,\hat\imath + 2\,\hat\jmath .$$

The coordinates are the two scalars that stretch \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) before adding them. This reading matters later. It says coordinates only mean something relative to a chosen pair of arrows, the basis. Choose a different pair and the same arrow is described by different numbers.

Linear combinations and span

Given two vectors \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\), any expression

$$a\,\vec v + b\,\vec w$$

is a linear combination of them. Let \(a\) and \(b\) range over all real numbers and the tips sweep out a set called the span of \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\). Only three things can happen:

  • Different directions: the span is the whole plane. The lines through \(\vec v\) and through \(\vec w\) act like a slanted coordinate grid, and every point has a parallelogram route back to the origin along those two directions.
  • Same line: if \(\vec w\) is a multiple of \(\vec v\) (and they are not both zero), every combination stays on that one line, so the span is the line.
  • Both zero: the span is the origin alone.

Things to try
  • Scatter random combinations with \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\) in different directions. The dots spread over the whole plane.
  • Press "Line w up with v" and scatter again. Every dot lands on one line, and the cyan grid collapses onto it.
  • Drag the yellow tip around. For independent \(\vec v,\vec w\) the sliders find the one pair \((a, b)\) that reaches it.

Redundancy: linear dependence

On the same line, one of the two vectors adds nothing: anything you can reach with both, you could already reach with one. Vectors like that are called linearly dependent. The precise definition, which works for any number of vectors, is that some combination with scalars not all zero gives the zero vector:

$$a\,\vec v + b\,\vec w = \vec 0 \quad\text{with } (a, b) \neq (0, 0).$$

If \(b \ne 0\) this rearranges to \(\vec w = -\tfrac{a}{b}\vec v\), which is "\(\vec w\) lies on \(\vec v\)'s line". If no such combination exists, the vectors are linearly independent: each one adds a new direction.

Basis: just enough vectors

A basis of a space is a set of linearly independent vectors that spans it. In the plane, any two vectors pointing in different directions form a basis, and every vector \(\vec p\) then has exactly one pair of coordinates \((a, b)\) with \(\vec p = a\vec v + b\vec w\). Existence is the span argument above. Uniqueness is independence: if two pairs worked,

$$a\vec v + b\vec w = a'\vec v + b'\vec w \;\Longrightarrow\; (a - a')\vec v + (b - b')\vec w = \vec 0,$$

and independence forces \(a = a'\) and \(b = b'\). That is why dragging the yellow tip in the visualizer always moves the sliders to a single answer, until \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\) line up and the answer either disappears or stops being unique.

So "the coordinates of a vector" always means "its coordinates in some basis". The standard basis \(\hat\imath, \hat\jmath\) is just the default. The next chapter asks what happens when you move \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) themselves.
